    Symbol guide

    Seven symbols, from at least seven traditions, written in one line: eiπ + 1 = 0. Each entry gives what the symbol means and where its written form came from.

    The seven

    e
    Euler's number, 2.71828…The limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ: Jacob Bernoulli, Basel, 1683. The letter: Euler, in a manuscript of 1727–28 and a letter of 1731; in print in his Mechanica, 1736.
    i
    The imaginary uniti² = −1. Met by Cardano (1545), used in earnest by Bombelli (1572), named imaginary by Descartes (1637), given the letter i by Euler (1777).
    π
    Circumference over diameterMeasured in Babylon, Egypt, Greece, China, India and the Islamic world. The Greek letter: William Jones, 1706; made standard by Euler.
    +
    PlusFirst printed in Johannes Widmann's arithmetic, Leipzig, 1489. Probably a shortening of Latin et, "and".
    1
    OneWritten by every counting culture. This shape descends from Indian Brahmi numerals through Arabic forms to Latin Europe.
    =
    EqualsRobert Recorde, The Whetstone of Witte, 1557: two parallel lines of one length.
    0
    ZeroInvented as a placeholder in Babylon and by the Maya, and as a number in its own right in India (Brahmagupta, 628). The word comes through Arabic ṣifr, "empty".

    The invisible eighth

    xⁿ
    Raised exponentA position, not a mark. Descartes, La Géométrie, 1637.

    On the plates

    t, θ
    Angle, in radianse^(it) is the point at angle t on the unit circle. A full turn is 2π.
    cos, sin
    Cosine and sinee^(it) = cos t + i sin t. "Sine" comes from Sanskrit jyā (bowstring) through Arabic jība to Latin sinus.
    helix
    The circle, stretched through timePlot e^(it) with t as height and the circle becomes a helix. The history plates use the same trick: angle is longitude, height is the year.
