Votes as rain

U.S. House votes from the 2026 election, read from official state results files. Each drop is real counted votes falling on its county, and the fluid equations do the rest.

The rain

The land is impermeable: nothing soaks in. Navy water is Democratic votes, orange water is Republican votes, and purple is where they have met. Where the water goes after it lands is physics, not politics: the stirring is random, and state lines don't hold it back unless you raise barriers.

What is being solved

The incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in two dimensions, on a 768 by 480 grid: advection, viscosity and a pressure solve that keeps the flow very nearly divergence-free. The coast is a no-slip wall. The two colors are passive dyes carried by the flow, with a small molecular diffusivity. Tested against the exact decay of Taylor-Green vortices; the grid adds some numerical viscosity of its own, shown beside the slider. The advection scheme doesn't conserve dye by itself, so each color's total is rescaled twice a second to match the votes that have fallen.

Big whorls, lesser whorls. Each drop stirs an eddy, larger for counties with more votes. In two dimensions eddies tend to merge into bigger ones, the reverse of the three-dimensional cascade in Richardson's rhyme, while the dye is stretched into ever finer filaments. Those filaments are the lesser whorls you see, and viscosity and diffusivity finish them off.

Mixing length. The chart shows the typical width of the blue and red streaks, from the dye field: the square root of its variance over its mean squared gradient. Distances are rough, since the map is projected and Alaska and Hawaii are inset. Stirring thins the streaks and pulls it down; diffusion widens them and pushes it up, so it can move either way. Once the water is evenly purple there are no streaks left to measure.

Barriers. They are walls in the solver, built the same way as the coast, so each state or county has to mix only its own water. County lines are the finest boundaries available here: the page has no district or precinct outlines. At county scale the cells are small, so whorls are small too, and walls take up a noticeable share of the grid.

Not modeled. Terrain, depth, evaporation, weather and three-dimensional flow. Drops come from House races with both a Democrat and a Republican on the ballot, so third-party votes are left out. All the rain here is one snapshot falling at once; it isn't the order the votes were counted in.

What if?

Same ballots, different rules, for the states in this edition only. Under other rules people would vote differently, parties would run differently, and some of this would need a constitutional amendment, so it shows what the rule does to these ballots, not what would have happened. It says nothing about who controls Congress.

What if it were all on a blockchain?

Same totals. A ledger changes how the count is recorded, not how it's added. This is a real SHA-256 chain, built in your browser with one block for each state's House count. Change a block and it stops matching its fingerprint.

So what did it prove? That this list hasn't changed since your browser wrote it. Not that the numbers going in were right, that each voter was real and voted once, or that anyone's ballot stayed secret. Those are the hard parts of an election, and a chain doesn't touch them. That's why the National Academies' 2018 report advised against internet voting, blockchain included, in favor of paper ballots people can check and audits of them.

Where the numbers come from, and their limits

Sources. Each state's own results file, linked in the table above. County rows are added up and, where the state publishes a district total, checked against it. County codes come from tonmcg's county results and outlines from us-atlas. This page isn't affiliated with any election office.

Which states. Those whose official files hold House votes by county and could be read by a plain script. Other states may join as their results are published. Indiana is held back for now because its file shows some counties with nothing counted, which would mislead.

A snapshot. Counting continues in some states, especially Washington and California, so these totals will grow and some leads may change. This page doesn't update by itself.

Not here. No probabilities, projections or race calls. Florida doesn't list unopposed candidates in its file, so one Florida seat has no count. Races with two candidates of one party, as California's top-two system can produce, count toward the totals but send down no rain.
