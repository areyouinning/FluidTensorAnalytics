Does the future change the past?

Send one photon into a split path. Whether it behaves like a wave or like a particle seems to depend on a switch you can flip after it has already gone through. John Wheeler dreamt this up in 1978 to make the point sting. It looks like the future reaching back to fix the past. It isn't — and the apparatus itself shows why.

One photon, two paths

A beamsplitter sends a single photon two ways at once. Mirrors bring the paths back together at a second beamsplitter, and two detectors wait beyond it. With that second beamsplitter in, the two paths interfere: turn the phase and the photon lands at D1 or D2 in a smooth fringe — wave behavior, and you cannot say which path it took. Take the second beamsplitter out and the paths never recombine: it's 50/50, the phase does nothing, and the detector that fires tells you which path the photon was on — particle behavior.

Decide too late

Here is Wheeler's twist. Wait until the photon is already past the first beamsplitter — already "committed," you'd think, to being a wave or a particle — and only then choose whether to put the second beamsplitter in. Flip the control above to Choose late and watch: the numbers don't budge. The photon never had to decide anything early, because quantum mechanics keeps no secret fact about "which path" for you to catch it on. Only the arrangement the photon actually meets at the end sets the statistics.

So does the future change the past?

No. The cleanest way to see it is the quantum eraser, where the which-path clue is written onto a second, entangled photon instead of thrown away. Look at all the signal photons together and the screen is flat — no fringes, whatever you later do to the partner. Nothing you choose afterward puts fringes into that whole pattern.

What a late "erase" buys you is a way to sort. Measure the partner so the which-path clue is gone, and its two outcomes let you split the very same photons into two groups — one a fringe, the other its mirror-image anti-fringe — that add straight back to the flat total. The correlation was always there, baked in when the two photons were born together. Reading the partner late just tells you how to file the photons; it adds no information to the past and sends none backward.

That is the whole resolution. The "choice" never changes what happened; it changes which correlation you are allowed to read out. A fringe you can only see by sorting with information from the other photon is a correlation, not a message, and correlations don't need a direction in time. No signal travels backward, and none is needed.

The delayed-choice experiment is John Wheeler's (1978); the eraser is Scully and Drühl (1982), realised many times since. The interferometer and eraser here are exact single-photon statistics — P(D1) = sin²(φ/2) with the second beamsplitter in, 50/50 without — computed in your browser.
