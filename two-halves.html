Can you fix both copies at once?

You have a machine that rewrites one letter of a gene. It does not fit in the truck. So you send it as two halves, in two trucks, and hope both reach the same cell. Then it has to do the job twice, because the cell has two copies.

It's a puzzle about coincidence, and coincidences stack badly. Everything below is a made-up model of a real shape, not biology or advice — the last section says what it leaves out.

Meet the gene editor

Both halves, same cell

A cell is fixed only where half A and half B overlap, and arrivals are independent. Half B packs worse — here each B vehicle carries 35% the weight of an A — so an even split is the wrong guess.

Send more of the weaker half — but not in full proportion. Both terms saturate, so the best split sits between even and proportional.

Then do it twice

A full machine works on each of the cell's two copies: corrected, scarred for good, or no change (try again). Even at 76% per copy you need both, and 0.76² is 57%. The missing 43% is mostly cells with one copy fixed and one ruined — spent, and unrecoverable. That squaring is a ceiling no dose can lift.

Why not just send more

Dose isn't free: more vehicles reach more cells but harm more too, so yield peaks in the middle. You can also come back — over six steps, held halves decay (45% survive each step) and late vehicles work less well (75% each step).

So the answer has a shape: three front-loaded doses, each lopsided toward the weaker half, easing as it goes (the early doses already stocked half B, so by the third dose half A is scarcer). A fourth dose adds nothing.

The puzzle

Six steps. At each one, choose a dose and how to divide it between the halves; zero is a reasonable answer for most of them. Your score is the fraction of the cells you started with that end up alive with both copies corrected. Higher is better.

Nothing is sampled and nothing is uploaded. The score is the exact answer from a 24-state chain over what a cell holds and how its two copies stand, run in your browser as you drag. A step you leave empty still passes: a machine already in the cell works again, and held halves decay again.

What this leaves out

Every number above is invented. I picked round values that make the tradeoffs visible, not measurements of anything. The model is worth playing with for its shape; it is worth nothing as a prediction.

The real thing is far harder: reaching the right cells in an organ that resists them, an immune system that remembers, measuring success in tissue you can't sample, and whether editing an adult helps at all. None of that is arithmetic. What does carry over is the squaring: whenever two independent rare things must happen in one place and either failure is permanent, your ceiling is the square of the single-event rate, and the best plan front-loads and stays lopsided.

The chain, the parameters, the diagrams and the words here are mine, and the parameters are fiction. The one real fact borrowed from biology is the shape of the problem: a precise single-letter editor is large enough that splitting it across two delivery vehicles is a standard workaround, and someone homozygous has two copies to fix.
