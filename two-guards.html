Riddles

What one question gets you out?

There are two doors. One leads out and the other does not. Each door has a guard. One guard always tells the truth and the other always lies, and you do not know which is which. You may ask one guard one yes-or-no question. What do you ask?

Try four questions

Each question goes to the guard at the left door. The table shows all four possible worlds and the answer you would hear in each.

Why the obvious question fails

Ask “Is your door the way out?” and you get a yes or a no. A truth-teller says yes when the door is out. A liar says yes when it is not. The same answer turns up in both kinds of world, so you have an answer and no reason to believe it.

The trick

Route the question through both guards, or through the same guard twice.

Take question 3. If you happen to ask the truth-teller, the other guard is the liar, whose answer would be false, and the truth-teller reports it faithfully. If you happen to ask the liar, the other guard’s answer would be true, and the liar flips it. Either way what you hear is false. A reliably wrong answer is as useful as a reliably right one: hear “yes” and take the other door.

Question 4 uses one guard twice. Asked about their own answer, a truth-teller just repeats it. A liar lies about their lie, and two lies cancel. Either way “yes” means this door.

Flipping twice

An answer passed through a liar is flipped, and flipping twice restores it. In logic this is double negation: not-not-P is P. In computing it is the same arithmetic as adding two bits and keeping only the last digit, which is called XOR.

You cannot find out who is lying with one question, and you do not need to.

A knights-and-knaves puzzle of the kind Raymond Smullyan collected; this two-door version is widely known from the 1986 film Labyrinth. The table is computed from the rules, not written in. The words and diagram are mine.
