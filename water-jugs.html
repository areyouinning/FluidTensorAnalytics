Riddles

Can two jugs measure any amount?

You have a 3-liter jug, a 5-liter jug, a tap and a drain. No markings. Measure out exactly 4 liters. You may fill a jug to the brim, empty it, or pour one into the other until the first runs dry or the second is full. That is all.

Try it

Pour for yourself before you press Solve. Then change the jugs and the target, and look for a pair where the riddle cannot be done.

Why some targets are impossible

Every move adds or removes a whole jugful from the total water you are holding, so the total is always some number of A-jugs plus some number of B-jugs, where the numbers can be negative (a jugful poured down the drain). With jugs of 6 and 4, every such total is even, so 3 liters is out of reach for good. In general the amounts you can make are exactly the multiples of the greatest common divisor of the two sizes, as long as they fit in a jug.

That the multiples of the gcd are all reachable, and nothing else is, is Bézout’s identity: for whole numbers a and b there are whole numbers m and n with m·a + n·b = gcd(a, b). For 3 and 5 the gcd is 1, and 2·5 − 2·3 = 4: fill the 5 twice, tip out the 3 twice. That is the six-move solution, read straight off the equation.

The path is a billiard shot

Watch the grid while it solves. Filling and emptying slide the dot along an edge. Pouring keeps the total fixed, so the dot runs down a diagonal until it hits a wall. The whole solution is a ball bouncing around a table, and it only ever lands on lattice points. Keep filling the same jug and pouring into the other and the ball visits every reachable amount in turn, which is the Euclidean algorithm for the gcd played out in water.

The riddle is centuries old; the gcd rule is Bézout’s (1779). “Solve” runs a breadth-first search over the states, so its answer is a shortest one. The words and diagram are mine.
