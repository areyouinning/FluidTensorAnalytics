Chance

Why run a forecast a hundred times?

Because the second run disagrees with the first, and how much they disagree is itself the forecast.

The orange line is three equations from Edward Lorenz's 1963 toy model of the atmosphere. The blue lines are the same equations, started a tiny bit differently. Same physics, no randomness.

That is chaos, and you can't engineer around it. Measuring the start ten times better buys you only a little more time. So stop asking for the one right line and ask what the pack is doing.

From a pile to an answer

Now the useful part. Run the forecast a hundred times and you can answer the question a reservoir operator has: how likely is it to pass this line? A single best guess can only say yes or no.

Where do the extra runs come from?

You can't always rerun the physics. Rivers have short records and long memories, so people make ensembles out of what they've got. Three favorites:

Reuse the record

The bootstrap treats your few years of data as the whole world and draws from them with replacement. Some years come up twice, some not at all. Do that a thousand times and the wobble in the average tells you how far to trust it.

Find similar years

Nearest neighbors asks which past years looked like this one, then treats what those years did next as the forecast. No equations at all, which is either its charm or its problem.

Fix the model's habits

A climate model can have the right story and the wrong numbers: rain too often, too gently. Quantile mapping keeps the story and replaces the numbers. A model's 90th-percentile day becomes the record's 90th-percentile day.

How to catch an ensemble lying

Any spread looks scientific, so you have to check it. Here's a lovely trick: for each past forecast, rank the truth among the members. With nine members there are ten slots. If the truth really behaves like one more member, every slot gets the same share.

Bootstrap: Efron, 1979. Rank histograms: Anderson, Hamill and Talagrand, independently, in the 1990s. Every data set on this page except the Lorenz runs is made up, to show the method.
