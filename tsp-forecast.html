Will the travelling salesman fall next?

In 1865 Jules Verne fired three men at the Moon from Florida, a century early. He got there by taking parts that already existed and following the arithmetic. This page tries the same thing on a smaller target: the oldest speed record in computer science.

This is a forecast, not news. Written 17 November 2026. Nothing below the heading “The forecast” has happened. The last section says how to score it.

Play it first

Tap the cities in the order you would visit them. The route closes itself when every city is used. Then see how close you came to the shortest tour, and press Unroll to lay your tour, the shortest tour and the greedy tour end to end on one ruler.

The record

A salesman must visit n cities once each and come home by the shortest route. Trying every tour takes (n − 1)!/2 attempts. In 1962 Michael Held and Richard Karp, and separately Richard Bellman, found a way that takes about n² · 2n steps. Sixty-four years on, that is still essentially the record. A 2024 paper by Mihail Stoian shaved a little off, but nobody has reached 1.99n. Whether anyone can is a famous open question.

The claimed fall of the Exponential Time Hypothesis does not settle it. That result is about a logic puzzle, and translating tours into it adds too many variables. But it shows a method, and the method travels.

Part one, which exists: cancellation

Euler’s bridges are easy because crossing every bridge once comes down to counting odd corners. Visiting every city once has no such shortcut. In 2010 Andreas Björklund found the next best thing for maps with no distances, where the only question is whether a tour exists. He added up all closed walks, good and bad, in such a way that every walk that revisits a city is cancelled by a partner. Only true tours survive. That takes about 1.657n steps, well under 2n. Distances are what break it.

Part two, the missing piece: temperature

Here is an old physicist’s trick for putting distances back. Give each tour of length L the weight e−L/T, where T is a temperature, and add up the weights of all tours. Call the total Z. When T is high, every tour counts about the same. As T falls, long tours fade, and in the cold limit −T · ln Z is exactly the length of the shortest tour.

So finding the best tour is a counting problem in disguise, and counting is what the algebra of part one is good at. The catch is cost. Today, counting weighted tours takes time that grows with the size of the distances themselves, and going cold makes that worse.

The forecast

The ETH proof never computed its answer directly. It computed a deformed version at a few settings of a dial where the problem fell apart into small groups, then interpolated back to the setting it wanted. I predict the same move works here, with temperature as the dial: evaluate the tour sum at a handful of warm temperatures, where a cancellation count is cheap, and interpolate to the cold limit.

The third part is the splitting itself: cities sorted into groups joined by a few shared measurements, and the groups multiplied together the way a tensor train is. That is the step the ETH papers supply.

The Florida details. The first result will be ugly: about 1.99n, a hair under the line. Within a year it drops toward Björklund’s 1.657n. It comes from an AI model working with the people who built part one, in Lund, Aalto and Utrecht, and it is checked in Lean. It will not be subexponential, it will not show P = NP, and it will change nothing for delivery vans: practical solvers already find exact tours through tens of thousands of cities by other means.

Verne put his launch site in the right state and the wrong town. I expect to get the method right and the constant wrong.

How this could be wrong

The likeliest failure is the interpolation. It may only work when distances are small whole numbers, which is already known to be easier, and leave general tours at 2n. Second, the ETH proofs are still under review; if they fail, the template goes with them. Third, someone may prove that 2n is a true barrier under an assumption that survives, such as the Stearns–Hunt hypothesis. Any of those would be worth a page of its own.

How to score it

  1. The record falls. By 31 December 2028, a paper gives an algorithm for the travelling salesman problem with arbitrary distances that runs in cn steps for some c below 2, and experts accept it.
  2. The method. It counts tours algebraically with cancellation, and reaches the shortest tour through a deformation or interpolation, not by search.
  3. The second step. Within a year of the first paper, the base drops below 1.8.
  4. The authorship. An AI model is credited with the core argument, and a machine-checked proof accompanies it.
