Riddles

Who keeps the zebra?

Ada, Bo, Cy and Dee each live in a different home, keep a different pet and have a different favourite colour. Nobody tells you who keeps the zebra. You get eight clues, such as “the camel lives in the shack”, and the rest is deduction.

Solve it

Tap a cell to cycle through its choices. Clues turn green when they hold and red when you have broken one.

The grid is three matrices

Look at the small “who × home” grid once a column is filled in properly. It has exactly one dot in each row and one in each column: every person has one home, and every home has one person. A grid like that is a permutation matrix, and the whole puzzle is the hunt for three of them.

Now look at clue 1. “The camel lives in the shack” does not name a person at all. It links a pet to a home through whoever has both. That is what the fourth grid shows: line up “who × home” with “who × pet”, match them on the person, and you get “home × pet”. Matching two tables on a shared column is exactly what multiplying two matrices does. Clue 1 says one particular cell of that product holds a dot.

The clues are a tiny ontology

Before you can even read the clues you need to know what kinds of things exist and how they may relate. There are four kinds: people, homes, pets, colours. There are relations between them: lives-in, keeps, likes. And there are rules about the relations: each person lives in exactly one home, and no home is shared. Write those down and you have an ontology, which is the name for that list of kinds, relations and rules.

The ontology is what makes eight short sentences enough. Without “no home is shared”, clue 4 would tell you almost nothing. With it, ruling Dee out of the shack pushes someone else in. Databases and knowledge graphs work the same way: the facts are few, and the rules about what a fact can be do most of the deducing.

The zebra puzzle first appeared in Life International in 1962; it is often credited to Einstein, without evidence. This smaller one is mine, and a brute-force search over all 13,824 assignments confirms it has exactly one answer.
