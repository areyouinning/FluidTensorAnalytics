Can you cross every bridge once?

The river Pregel ran through Königsberg in two branches around an island, and seven bridges joined the four pieces of land. People there asked whether you could take a walk that crossed every bridge exactly once. In 1736 Leonhard Euler answered no, and explained why so plainly that the explanation turned into a branch of mathematics.

Try it

Pick a piece of land to start on, then tap a bridge next to you to cross it. Crossed bridges turn orange and get a number. You don't have to finish where you started.

However you go, you run out of road at six. Try starting in each of the four places. Six is always the best.

Why it can't be done

Euler noticed that the shapes don't matter, and neither do the lengths. All that matters is which land each bridge joins. Switch the figure above to Graph: each land becomes a dot and each bridge a line between two dots.

Now follow a walk through any land that isn't where you start or finish. You come in over one bridge and go out over another. Every visit uses up two bridges, so a land in the middle of the walk needs an even number of them. Only the start and the end can have an odd number.

Count Königsberg's bridges. The island has 5, and the other three lands have 3 each. That is four lands with an odd count, and a walk has room for at most two. So no walk crosses all seven, and it isn't a matter of trying harder.

The rule works the other way too, though Euler's paper didn't fully prove it. That proof came later, from Carl Hierholzer, published in 1873. If you can get from any land to any other, and none or exactly two lands have an odd number of bridges, then a walk over every bridge once exists. With two odd lands it runs from one to the other. With none, it ends where it began.
