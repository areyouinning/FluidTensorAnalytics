Riddles

When should you stop looking?

You are floating a river with twenty campsites along it, in no particular order of quality. You can pull over at any one, but you cannot paddle back upstream to a site you passed. Stop too early and you take a mediocre spot without knowing better. Wait too long and the best one is behind you. When do you stop?

Look first, then leap

The best rule has two parts. Drift past the first few sites only looking, to learn what good means on this river. After that, take the first site that beats everything you have seen. The only choice is how long to look.

Thirty-seven percent, twice

Slide the cutoff and watch the curve. Look at nothing and you take the first site: a 1-in-20 shot. Look at almost everything and the best has usually gone by. The peak is at about 37% of the sites, and the chance of success there is also about 37%.

Both numbers are 1/e, where e ≈ 2.718. For a long river, looking at a fraction x of the sites wins with probability −x ln x, and that is largest at x = 1/e, where it equals 1/e. The surprise is that the odds do not shrink as the river gets longer: with a million campsites the same rule still finds the single best one more than a third of the time.

Mathematicians call this optimal stopping. It was first posed in the 1950s as a problem about hiring, and the same sum applies to apartments, parking spots and anything else you must accept or pass on the spot.

The 1/e rule is classical; Lindley published a solution in 1961. The curve is the exact formula and the trips are simulated in your browser. The words and diagram are mine.
