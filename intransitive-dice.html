Is there a best die?

Here are four dice with odd numbers on their faces. Pick any one you like. I will pick one of the others, and in a long run of rolls mine will come up higher two times out of three. It doesn't matter which you pick.

Play me

Choose a die. Then we roll, and the higher number takes the roll. Eleven rolls make a match. Under each die are its six faces, so nothing is hidden.

If you pick first, the best you can do is win one roll in three, and a match of eleven about 12% of the time. If I pick first, you can always find the die that beats mine, and the 2/3 is yours.

Every die has a beater

The faces are A: 4 4 4 4 0 0, B: 3 3 3 3 3 3, C: 6 6 2 2 2 2 and D: 5 5 5 1 1 1. Each pair is a short sum.

  • A beats B: B always shows 3, so A wins whenever it shows a 4. That is 4 faces in 6, or 2/3.
  • B beats C: C only gets past 3 when it shows a 6. That is 2 faces in 6, so B wins 2/3.
  • C beats D: a 6 wins outright, a third of the time. A 2 wins only against D's 1, which is half of D. So C wins 1/3 + 2/3 × 1/2 = 2/3.
  • D beats A: a 5 wins outright, half the time. A 1 wins only against A's 0, a third of A. So D wins 1/2 + 1/2 × 1/3 = 2/3.

So the arrows go round in a loop, A to B to C to D and back to A. "Beats" works like rock, paper, scissors here, not like "taller than". There is no top of the order, and the average doesn't settle it either: C has the highest average, 10/3, and it still loses to B.

Roll two and the arrows turn around

James Grime made a set of five dice with two loops in it. With one die each, Red beats Blue beats Olive beats Yellow beats Magenta beats Red, round the outside of the pentagon below. A second loop runs through the star in the middle.

Now roll two of the same die each and add them up. The whole outer loop turns around. Red beat Blue 7 times in 12 with one die. With two each, Blue beats Red 85 times in 144. The star mostly keeps its direction, except Olive against Red, which tips over to Red by a hair, 671 to 625 out of 1,296.

That gives you a nastier trick than going second. Let your friend choose a die and whether you both roll one or two. Then you choose. Every die has something that beats it either way.

The four dice are Bradley Efron's, made famous by Martin Gardner in 1970, and the five-die set is James Grime's. The game, the diagrams and these words are new. Every probability on the page is counted exactly by listing all the ways the dice can land, with no sampling. The match chances add up the ways to take six or more of eleven rolls.
