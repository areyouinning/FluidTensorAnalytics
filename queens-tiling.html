Queens Tiling Challenge

Cover a 2025 × 2025 grid with as few rectangles as you can, leaving exactly one square empty in every row and column. Problem 6 of the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad says the answer is 2112.

This challenge adds one rule: the empty squares must be non-attacking queens, so no two share a diagonal either. That changes the answer on some small boards. On the 2025 board, I don't know what it does, and I haven't found anyone who does.

What the queens rule costs

On most small boards, nothing. On 8, 9 and 11, it costs extra tiles. These come from searching every queens placement on each board.

BoardWithout queensWith queensExtraPlacements that reach it

Placements are counted up to rotation and reflection. The without-queens column uses the proved formula n + ⌈2√n⌉ − 3.

Why 2025 is hard

The placements that reach the olympiad's bound are sheared lattices: a bands of b rows, with the columns interleaved. Such a lattice is non-attacking exactly when gcd(b+1, a−1) = 1 and gcd(b−1, a+1) = 1. Because 2025 is odd, every factor pair is odd, so both of those gcds are even and every lattice fails.

Nearby boards are easy

A 42 × 48 lattice gives 2016 queens in 2103 tiles, and a 39 × 52 lattice gives 2028 queens in 2116 tiles. Both equal the answer without the queens rule. Deleting holes from the larger one, inserting holes into the smaller one, joining lattices in blocks and repairing the 45 × 45 lattice all failed to beat 3039 on the 2025 board.

Rules

  1. A placement is 2025 whole numbers. The k-th is the column, from 1 to 2025, of the queen in row k.
  2. Every column is used once and no two queens share a diagonal.
  3. Your score is the fewest rectangles covering every other square, computed exactly in your browser. Nothing is uploaded.
  4. Below 3039 beats the best so far. A score of 2112 would show the queens rule costs nothing on this board. A proof that some score is the minimum settles the question.

Problem source: IMO 2025, Problem 6. For the intuition behind 2112, see 3Blue1Brown's The last IMO problem AI could not solve. Tile counts use the rectilinear partition theorem: minimum rectangles = reflex vertices − maximum non-crossing chords − enclosed holes + 1, with the chord count found by bipartite matching.
