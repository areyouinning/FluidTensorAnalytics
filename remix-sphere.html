Random Points in a Sphere

Every method here puts every point inside the ball. Only two of the four put them there evenly, and a cloud of dots is a bad place to spot the difference.

The wasteful one

Pick a point in the cube around the ball. If it landed outside, throw it away and pick again. The ball fills π/6 of the cube, so about 47.6% of tries get thrown away, shown in coral. It costs 5.7 random numbers per point kept, and it is correct.

Two tempting repairs

Stretch a cube keeps every try: take the direction from the centre to a cube point, then pick a distance from 0 to 1. Plain angles picks a longitude, a latitude angle and a distance, each evenly. Both waste nothing, and both are wrong twice.

The first fault is the distance. A shell at radius r has area in proportion to r², so a shell near the centre holds far less room than one near the skin. Choosing r evenly gives each the same number of points, and the inner 46% of the radius, which is a tenth of the volume, gets 4.6 times its share. The left-hand bar in the chart shows it.

The second fault is direction, and it differs between the two. Directions taken from a cube are bunched toward its corners, because a cube has more volume out that way. Plain angles bunch at the poles, because every line of longitude meets there. Pick Stretch a cube, drag the Slice down and look straight at a cube face: the points lean toward the diagonals, faintly, since the crowded centre swamps it. The readout is plainer, with corner directions getting about 2.5 times as many points as face directions.

The fix

Corrected angles uses r = cbrt(u) and φ = acos(2v − 1), which are the inverses of how volume and area actually accumulate. All three crowding readouts sit near ×1. It costs three random numbers, one cube root and some trigonometry for each point.

Berry's punchline is the part worth keeping: the wasteful method is hard to beat. Its only cost is a loop and some multiplication. The fixes swap the retries for cube roots and trigonometry, which are not free either.

Reading the readouts

Each one counts the points on screen and compares with what an even scatter would give, so ×1 is correct, and it wobbles with each new set. Centre crowding is the innermost tenth of the volume. Pole crowding is the two 30° caps on the vertical axis. Corners over faces compares 20° caps around the cube's eight corner directions with caps around its six face directions.

After Random points in a sphere by Nick Berry, DataGenetics, January 2020, which has its own app and the code for each method. The rendering, the slice, the readouts and these words are new.
