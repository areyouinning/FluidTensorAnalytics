Wind where you actually are

A forecast gives one wind for a whole grid square, as if the ground under it were a tabletop. It isn't. This takes the real shape of the ground beneath you and works out what that wind has to become to get over it.

Temperatures are in Fahrenheit because that is how the forecast gets read out loud here; wind stays in metres per second to match the twin above it, where 10 m/s is a stiff breeze and 20 is a day to stay off a ladder.

Pick a place first, then throw.

The flight model is the one behind the site's Disc Flight Simulator, with the wind put where it belongs. That page treated wind as a sideways shove on the disc. Wind does not shove a wing, it changes the air it is moving through — so here it enters the relative airflow, and lift, drag and the angle of attack all follow from that. A headwind therefore makes the disc climb and float, which is what a headwind really does, rather than pushing it backwards.

The coefficients are fitted, not measured, and they differ from that page's. Its numbers landed a 25 m/s drive at 25 m — about a quarter of the real thing — so drag, lift, turn and fade were all refitted here against a throw you can pace out: 25 m/s at 12° carries 88 m in six seconds, an understable disc finishing right and an overstable one left. Treat it as a believable disc, not a measured one.

The column above you

The twin solves the air near the ground, where terrain decides things. Above that, the forecast carries its own profile: the wind a kilometre up is usually faster, and often pointing somewhere else entirely. Look up puts those layers over your camera, each drifting at the speed and bearing the forecast gives it.

In Colorado that view does a second job. A Peaks button appears, and the named summits go on the sky where the geometry puts them: bearing from spherical trigonometry, height above level from the summit's elevation minus the Earth's own curve. Over a hundred kilometres that curve drops the ground about 780 m away from your line of sight, and the air bends light back over roughly an eighth of it — leave both out and Pikes Peak from Denver lands half a degree high, a thumb's width off the real thing. A summit the twin's own terrain hides behind a nearer ridge is drawn hollow, because that is where the peak is rather than what you can see.

The old peak list was wrong, so this one is generated. The site's Front Range Peak Finder carries 52 hand-entered summits. Checked against the USGS national elevation model, most of those coordinates are not on their summits: the point listed for Chiefs Head Peak reads 825 m below the height beside it, Ypsilon Mountain 316 m below, Hagues Peak 280 m. A coordinate that far out is in a valley, and it throws the bearing as badly as the height — which is the one number a peak finder exists to get right. It had James Peak fourteen kilometres from James Peak. The list used here is built instead from every named Colorado summit in OpenStreetMap at or above 4,000 m, thinned so no two kept summits sit within 600 m of each other. That keeps Meeker beside Longs and Torreys beside Grays, which are two mountains to anyone looking at them, and drops the duplicate nodes that are not.

Seen from underneath, a layer of air is a ceiling. A ray leaving your eye at some angle above the horizon meets a layer at a distance set by that angle, so the same wind races overhead and crawls near the horizon — which is how moving cloud has always looked. The skyline drawn in the camera view comes from the same elevation data as the map, so it doubles as a check: if the drawn ridge doesn't sit on the real one, your phone's compass is off, not the wind.

Pressure levels can be underground. A forecast reports the 1000 and 925 hPa levels wherever they fall, which in Westminster at 1641 m means 142 m and 810 m above sea level — beneath your feet. Those are extrapolated numbers, not air, and the page drops any level that does not clear the local ground.

What it is doing

Three things, in order. It pulls the shape of the ground around your point from elevation tiles. It pulls a wind forecast for that point. Then it makes the second one obey the first.

Draping the forecast

A forecast says the wind is, say, 6 m/s from the west. Near the ground that cannot be true at every height: friction drags the lowest air to a near standstill, and the speed climbs logarithmically above it. How fast it climbs depends on what the ground is covered in. That shape is the neutral log profile, and it is where WindNinja starts too.

Making it obey the hill

Drape that profile over a hill and you get something impossible: air flowing into rock, and more air leaving some places than entered them. Air does not pile up. So we go looking for the nearest wind field that both avoids the rock and conserves mass.

"Nearest, subject to a constraint" is a minimisation, and its solution comes out as a single equation for one scalar — the same projection step that keeps a fluid simulation incompressible, run once, over a weather forecast. Solve it and you have corrected every arrow at once.

There is one knob, and it decides everything interesting. Making vertical adjustment cheap sends air over the hill. Making it expensive sends air around. Real air chooses based on how stably the atmosphere is layered: a cold, settled morning pins air to the valley floor, while a well-mixed afternoon lets it climb.

Drawing it

The map borrows its look from earth.nullschool.net: thousands of weightless particles dropped into the field, each carried along and leaving a fading trail, coloured by how fast it is going. Nothing about the picture is decorative — every trail is a path through the solved field, and where trails bunch and brighten is where the air is genuinely being squeezed.

What it is not

This is a toy built from the same ideas as a serious tool, which is not the same as being one. The honest list:

  • The fine levels are above the lowest ground, not above you. The solver's levels are a metre apart up to 10 m and ten apart up to 100, which is proper resolution where the profile bends hardest — but those heights are measured from the floor of the square, not from each column's own ground. Stand near the low point of the view and the metre-scale levels are at your feet. Stand on a summit three hundred metres above it and they are underground, and your air sits in the stretched part. Measuring from each column instead needs a terrain-following mesh, which is what WindNinja has and this does not.
  • It is still coarse sideways. Columns are a couple of hundred metres apart, and the ground is represented by switching whole cells off below the surface rather than by cutting them, which is blunt on a steep slope.
  • It is steady state. One forecast hour in, one wind field out. No gusts, no turbulence, no time.
  • No slope flow and no stability model. Real mountain air drains downhill on clear nights and climbs sunlit slopes by day. WindNinja models that; this does not. The over-or-around knob is yours to set by hand instead of being derived from the temperature profile.
  • The forecast is a point. A real downscaling run can take a whole gridded forecast as input. This takes one column and assumes it applies across the square.
  • The camera view is a backdrop, not a tracked scene. There is no visual tracking of any kind: the overlay is aimed purely by the phone's compass and tilt. Indoors, in a car, or near anything steel, a phone compass can be tens of degrees out, and the whole sky will be rotated with it. The drawn skyline is the check — if it doesn't sit on the real ridge, the heading is wrong, not the wind.
  • Layers aloft are the raw forecast, not the twin. The terrain solve applies near the ground. The air a kilometre up is shown exactly as the forecast gives it, undownscaled, which is honest for a layer that mostly ignores the hills anyway.
  • Elevation tiles are mixed quality. Good enough to look at and to shape a flow, not good enough to build anything on.
  • The radar is one still frame, and it is coarse. It is the newest composite RainViewer publishes — not a loop, and not a forecast of where the cell is going. The free tiles stop at zoom 7, which is roughly a kilometre per pixel, so the default 12 km square is about a dozen radar pixels across and will usually be one flat colour or empty. Widen Across to 30 km before expecting it to mean anything. Above zoom 7 that server returns a grey "Zoom in" card rather than an error, which draws like a solid overcast if you let it; the page checks for that card and reports missing radar instead.
  • The peak list is 409 named Colorado summits above 13,000 feet, taken from OpenStreetMap, which is not every bump on a skyline — an unlabelled peak may simply be below the cut. From the Front Range about 180 of them clear the horizon at once, far more than a screen can name, so the ones that get a label are chosen by how large they loom: angular height above level, which already combines elevation and distance. Carets stop after the forty biggest in view and names after fourteen. A peak you can see and cannot get a name for is losing a space contest, not missing.
  • Peak heights are OpenStreetMap's, not survey marks, and agree with published summit elevations to a few feet on the famous ones. The refraction constant is a standard-atmosphere average: on a cold morning over snow light bends about twice as hard and distant summits sit genuinely higher than drawn, and on a hot afternoon lower. Near peaks are unaffected either way.

Do not plan a flight, a burn, or a roof with it. Do use it to see why the wind at your back door is not the wind on the forecast.

About your location. Nothing here runs until you ask. If you press Use my location, your browser asks you first, and the coordinates go to public services to fetch the forecast and the ground tiles for that square — the same requests a weather page makes. The only thing kept is the last forecast, saved in your own browser so the page still has something to show if a service goes down; nothing leaves for anywhere else, and this site has no server to store it on. Typing a place name works identically and tells those services a town instead of a doorstep.
