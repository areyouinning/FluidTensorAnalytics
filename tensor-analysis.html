Tensors

What is a tensor, really?

A grid of numbers with more than two directions. That sounds like bookkeeping. The interesting part is what happens when you try to break one apart.

Each new direction lets you ask the data one more kind of question. A table of gauges by days can't tell you how the answer changes between climate models. A row of cubes can.

Mathematicians mean something stricter by "tensor": an object that transforms in a particular way when you change coordinates. Here, as in most data work, it means a many-directional array.

Slicing

Hold one direction still and you get a table. Hold two still and you get a list. Most of what you'd want to do with a tensor starts with one of these moves.

The simplest possible cube

Suppose three short lists explain everything. How big each site is, how flow changes by month, and how wet each year was. Then every value is one number from each list, multiplied.

A cube built this way has rank one. Real data doesn't look like this, because snowmelt and monsoon rain don't share a calendar. But it might look like a few of these added together.

Taking one apart

That guess is the CP decomposition: write the tensor as a sum of rank-one cubes. Below is a made-up data set of 10 sites, 12 months and 8 years, 960 numbers in all. I put three things in it: snowmelt at the high sites, a late-summer monsoon at the southern ones, and a steady base everywhere, plus a little noise.

Move the slider from 1 to 3 and the leftover drains away. Past 3 you are mostly fitting noise. Look at the curves underneath: with three components, nobody told it about June or August, and it finds both.

There is something odd here. Take a table apart into simple pieces and you can rotate those pieces into endless other answers that fit equally well. With three or more directions, the CP pieces are often the only answer, up to order and scale. Joseph Kruskal worked out the conditions in 1977. That is why people hope the pieces mean something physical. Hope is the right word: a unique answer can still be a wrong model.

Other ways to break it

Tucker keeps a small core tensor that says how the pieces mix, which fits more data and costs more. Tensor train writes the whole thing as a chain of small three-way pieces, and it keeps working when the number of directions gets large. The bars are on a log scale, so each step across is a factor of ten or more.

A party trick: filling holes

If a few patterns explain the data, the values you have should predict the ones you lack. So punch holes in it, fit around them, and read off the guesses. Sensors fail all the time, so this one comes up.

CP goes back to Hitchcock in 1927, and to Carroll and Chang and to Harshman, separately, in 1970. Tucker published his in 1966 and Oseledets the tensor train in 2011. The data in the last three figures is synthetic and described above.
