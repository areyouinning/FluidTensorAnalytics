Why is climate data a cube?

Because weather happens somewhere, at some time, and there is more than one kind of it. Three questions, three directions.

Here is a real slice: one month from MERRA-2, NASA's reconstruction of the atmosphere, as a 9 × 11 grid with four of its variables. Each layer is the same patch of the world, measuring something different.

It gets big fast

The full MERRA-2 grid is 576 × 361 and runs hourly since 1980. Each direction multiplies. The bars below are on a log scale, because on a normal one the first three would vanish.

The sizes are not the interesting part. The interesting part is that you can't loop over that. So you stop looping.

Let the shapes do the work

Array libraries have a rule called broadcasting. Line two shapes up from the right. Where one has a 1, it stretches to fit. Anything else has to match exactly.

The third case is the classic bug. The rule is simple enough to keep in your head, and it still gets everybody at least once.

One line, every grid cell

Einstein summation goes further: name each direction with a letter, and any letter that vanishes from the output gets summed over. Here is the correlation between every grid cell and one river, assuming both have been standardized:

corr = np.einsum('ijt,t->ij', field, river) / n_months

The t disappears, so time is summed away, and a map comes out. Shift the river by a few months and the map answers a better question: what happens here that shows up in the river later?

That speckle is why this needs care. Test thousands of cells against a short record and some will correlate by luck. A map like this suggests where to look. It doesn't prove anything.

Because each of these is one array operation and not a loop, the same line can run on a GPU through a library like CuPy, which works the same sum on many cells at once.

MERRA-2 is produced by NASA's Global Modeling and Assimilation Office. The first figure is their data. The correlation figure is synthetic.
