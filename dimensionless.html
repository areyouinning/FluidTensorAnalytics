Why do the numbers that matter have no units?

A ship model in a towing tank, a blood vessel and a river can all be the same flow, as long as a few unit-free numbers match. Finding those numbers is dimensional analysis. Keeping the equations short enough to see them is the job of Einstein's summation notation.

Units have to cancel

Every physical quantity is built from a few base dimensions: mass M, length L, time T and temperature Θ. Speed is L T⁻¹, viscosity is M L⁻¹ T⁻¹. Any honest law of nature keeps working when you switch from metres to feet, so it can only depend on combinations where all the units cancel.

That gives a counting rule, the Buckingham π theorem, published by Edgar Buckingham in 1914. If a problem involves n quantities built from r independent dimensions, it can be rewritten in terms of n − r unit-free groups. Pipe flow depends on density, speed, diameter and viscosity: 4 quantities, 3 dimensions, so 1 group. That group is the Reynolds number, and two pipes with the same Reynolds number behave alike, whatever their size or fluid.

Einstein's shorthand

Fluid equations are full of sums over directions. Einstein's convention, introduced in 1916, drops the summation signs: an index that appears twice in a term is summed over 1, 2 and 3. An index that appears once is free, and the equation holds for each of its values.

  • aᵢ bᵢ means a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃, the dot product. The repeated i is a dummy, and any letter would do.
  • Aᵢⱼ xⱼ is a matrix times a vector: one equation for each free i.
  • δᵢⱼ is 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise, so δᵢⱼ xⱼ = xᵢ.
  • εᵢⱼₖ is +1 when i, j, k run in the order 1 2 3 (or a rotation of it), −1 when they run backwards, and 0 if any index repeats. It writes the cross product as (a × b)ᵢ = εᵢⱼₖ aⱼ bₖ.
  • An index may appear at most twice in a term. aᵢ bᵢ cᵢ means nothing.

Navier–Stokes in two lines

Momentum and mass for an incompressible fluid take two short lines in index form. Pick a component and see them written out in full.

That last step is the whole argument of this page in one move. Scale the variables by the problem's own sizes, and the equations say that only the Reynolds number can tell two flows apart. Add gravity, a free surface, heat or rotation, and more groups appear. The table below is a guide to them.

The thresholds in the table are the usual textbook values, and real flows shift them with shape, roughness and disturbance. The tools, the table and these words are new.
