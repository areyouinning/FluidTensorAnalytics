How does rain become a flood?

Rain falls for a few hours. The river at the bottom of the valley rises for a day and takes two more to fall. The curve of its flow over time is called a hydrograph, and hydrologists build them from the rain, piece by piece, the way a synthesizer builds a sound. Here are two synths: a simple one with a few knobs, and a modular one you patch together.

The simple synth: the unit hydrograph

In 1932, LeRoy Sherman had an idea that still runs flood studies today. Suppose one millimetre of runoff, spread over the whole catchment, always reaches the outlet in the same shape of rise and fall. Call that shape the unit hydrograph. Two millimetres give the same shape twice as tall. Rain that keeps falling gives a stack of shapes, each started a little later. Adding up those shifted copies is called convolution.

The US Soil Conservation Service (now the NRCS) found a standard shape that fits many catchments. It peaks at a time Tp after the rain, and the peak is about 0.208·A ÷ Tp cubic metres a second for each millimetre of runoff, with the area A in km² and Tp in hours. Bigger catchments make bigger floods. Slower catchments, with a longer lag, make lower and later ones.

Not all the rain runs off. Some soaks into the ground. The SCS curve number CN says how much: 98 for a car park, around 75 for farmland, 40 to 60 for a forest on deep soil. Its storage is S = 25,400 ÷ CN − 254 mm. The first 0.2·S of rain all soaks in, and after that the runoff is Q = (P − 0.2S)² ÷ (P + 0.8S).

Try the same storm on a car park and on a forest: change the curve number from 98 to 50. Then try a short, sharp storm against a long, gentle one with the same total rain. A long lag smooths both into the same shape.

The modular synth

A modular synthesizer splits a sound into modules: an oscillator makes the tone, a filter shapes it, an envelope controls its loudness. You patch cables between them, and you can pull any module out to hear what it does. A catchment splits the same way.

  • Rain is the oscillator, the source.
  • Losses take out what soaks in, by the curve number or by a φ index: a steady soaking rate in mm an hour.
  • Hillslope is a Nash cascade: water drains through a chain of n identical leaky buckets, each emptying at a rate of its store ÷ k. One bucket gives a sharp rise and a long tail. More buckets give a rounder, later peak. Nash showed in 1957 that the result is a gamma curve, much like the SCS shape.
  • Channel is Muskingum routing, from the Muskingum River in Ohio in the 1930s. A flood wave takes K hours to travel down the channel and spreads out on the way. X between 0 and 0.5 sets how little it spreads: 0 is a reservoir that flattens it most, and 0.5 passes it on unchanged.
  • Baseflow is the river's steady flow from groundwater, there before the storm and after it.

Bypass everything except the rain and the flow is the storm itself, spiky and instant. Patch the modules back in one at a time and watch the flood get later, lower and longer. Neither the buckets nor the channel lose water: the area under the curve stays the same and only its shape changes. That is the whole trick of a flood plain and a reservoir. They do not stop the water, they spread it out in time.

Play a river

A hydrograph has the same shape as a note on a synthesizer: it rises (the attack), falls back fast (the decay), settles on a steady flow (the sustain) and drains away when the water stops (the release). Two more instruments take that literally.

  • Hydrograph Synth fits each note's envelope to a whole water year. The rising limb sets the attack, the peak recession the decay, baseflow the sustain, and the slow recession constant the release. Pick a snowmelt river, a flashy creek or a spring-fed stream, or load a year of real data from a USGS gage, and play it on a keyboard.
  • Hydrograph Modular is a patchable rack where streamflow is a voltage, with storms, a sequencer and drums that rain plays. Its bottom row is the modular synth on this page, rebuilt as audio filters you can patch cables through: losses, a Nash cascade and Muskingum routing. A linear reservoir is exactly a one-pole low-pass filter, so a hillslope is literally a filter on the rain. The Flood routing patch sends storms through all of it and lets the river bend the pitch.

Both are best with headphones or good speakers, on a computer. The rack is wide, so on a phone it shrinks to fit.
